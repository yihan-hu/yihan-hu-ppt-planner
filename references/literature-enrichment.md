## 1. Purpose

Use external academic literature to fill missing scientific context, not to overwrite the user's study.

Search when the supplied material is incomplete or when up-to-date prior evidence would materially improve the Background, rationale, Discussion, or visual evidence.

## 2. When to search

Search academic literature when one or more apply:

- Background/Introduction is missing or too thin;
- the research rationale cannot be established from user material alone;
- current state of evidence or a knowledge gap must be verified;
- a landmark prior result is needed;
- a previous estimate provides a useful comparator;
- Discussion needs concordant or conflicting evidence;
- biological plausibility or clinical relevance needs outside support;
- a scientific figure, conceptual framework, or prior-study result would improve the presentation;
- the user explicitly asks for literature enrichment.

Do not search broadly just because a disease or topic was mentioned.

## 3. Derive search needs from the study

First reconstruct the study scope. Then define specific literature needs such as:

- scientific context;
- established association;
- conflicting evidence;
- unresolved methodological gap;
- biological plausibility;
- clinical importance;
- landmark result;
- recent update;
- visual source.

Build queries from the combination of study question + exposure + outcome + population + gap.

Avoid textbook-style searches that expand into every aspect of the disease unless those aspects are required to understand the study.

## 4. Source strategy

Use source type according to the claim.

### Established context

Prefer authoritative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, major guidelines, or consensus documents.

### Specific numerical estimate

Prefer the original primary study. Preserve the study population, contrast, effect measure, and uncertainty needed to interpret the number.

### Landmark finding

Prefer the original landmark paper.

### Current evidence state

Prefer recent high-quality primary studies plus recent systematic reviews/meta-analyses when available.

### Controversy or gap

Search for both supporting and conflicting evidence. Do not construct a gap by cherry-picking papers that favor the user's hypothesis.

When technical details or scientific claims are being verified, rely on primary research papers or other authoritative original sources whenever feasible.

## 5. Build the background evidence map

Before turning literature into slides, organize only the evidence that serves the study rationale.

For each candidate background claim, capture:

- claim;
- narrative function;
- source;
- source type;
- confidence;
- presentation priority;
- possible visual.

Useful narrative functions include:

- establish disease or exposure burden;
- establish known association;
- show clinical relevance;
- introduce a necessary mechanism;
- demonstrate uncertainty;
- show conflicting evidence;
- expose a methodological limitation in prior work;
- motivate the current research question.

Background should usually move toward:

`Why this matters -> What is known -> What remains unresolved -> Why prior evidence is insufficient -> What this study asks`

Keep only the minimum prerequisite knowledge the audience needs.

## 6. Validate the knowledge gap

Be conservative with novelty language.

Do not write "no previous studies" or "first study" unless the source or a sufficiently strong search supports it.

When certainty is lower, prefer language such as:

- evidence remains limited;
- findings have been inconsistent;
- the relationship remains uncertain;
- few studies have addressed this question;
- prior studies were limited by specific design constraints.

If the search is incomplete, say so rather than presenting a strong novelty claim.

## 7. Use previous-study results

External numerical results can be useful when they establish the rationale or a meaningful comparison.

For a previous-study estimate, preserve as applicable:

- study population;
- exposure/comparator;
- outcome;
- effect measure;
- confidence/credible interval;
- follow-up or time window;
- source.

Do not show only a p-value.

If a prior study is methodologically weak but narratively relevant, present the limitation that makes the current study necessary.

## 8. Use external figures and images

Search for external visuals only when they add scientific value.

Possible external visuals include:

- disease-course diagrams;
- conceptual frameworks;
- epidemiological trends;
- forest plots;
- risk curves;
- landmark-study figures;
- clinical or biological schematics.

Distinguish two categories.

### Scientific evidence figures

Examples: forest plots, Kaplan-Meier curves, risk curves, statistical charts.

Preserve axes, labels, data encoding, groups, uncertainty, and scientific implication. Prefer preserve/crop/annotate over decorative alteration.

If exact numerical data are available from a reliable source, creating a new chart from those values may be preferable to copying an unsuitable figure.

Do not infer exact values from a screenshot.

### Conceptual illustrations

Examples: mechanisms, anatomy, disease pathways, conceptual models.

A new schematic can be created when the scientific meaning is preserved. Do not make a near-copy of a protected artistic illustration.

### Reuse and attribution

Always retain source attribution. Prefer openly licensed/reusable material when practical. If reuse rights are unclear, prefer a new chart from reported data or a new conceptual schematic rather than copying a protected illustration.

## 9. Enrich the Discussion

When the user's Discussion is incomplete, search for:

- concordant studies;
- conflicting studies;
- meta-analytic context;
- plausible mechanisms;
- clinically relevant implications;
- methodological explanations for disagreement.

Do not search only for agreement.

Build Discussion as:

`Own finding -> relation to prior evidence -> plausible explanation -> major limitation/uncertainty -> implication`

Clearly label external mechanism evidence as external evidence rather than the study's own result.

## 10. Provenance and citation rules

Keep user results, external literature, and planning inference visibly separate.

When external literature is used in the user-facing plan, cite the supporting source near the claim. Prefer precise attribution over a generic bibliography.

Do not attribute a primary-study number to a review merely because the review mentions it if the primary source can be identified reliably.

Do not invent missing bibliographic details.

If internet or source access is unavailable, state what could not be verified and proceed only with the material that is supported.

## 11. Literature audit

Check:

- Did the search answer a specific presentation need?
- Does the Background lead to the research question rather than become a literature review?
- Are original sources used for specific numerical claims when feasible?
- Is conflicting literature represented when material?
- Are novelty claims appropriately cautious?
- Are external results clearly distinguished from user results?
- Are external visuals attributed and scientifically faithful?
- Does every external element earn its space in the presentation?
